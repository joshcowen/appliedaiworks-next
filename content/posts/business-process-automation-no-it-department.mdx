The business process automation solutions that work best for trades and service businesses with no IT department are Make.com for connecting apps and triggering workflows, Claude for drafting estimates, follow-up emails, and job summaries, Otter.ai for turning site walkthroughs into written notes, and Jobber or ServiceTitan for automating scheduling and customer communication. A plumbing company with eight employees can set up a working job-follow-up automation in Make.com in about two hours, with no developer, no IT ticket, and no monthly retainer to a software firm. The tools exist. The question is knowing which ones to connect and in what order.

## Why Most Automation Advice Fails Trades Owners

Most content about business process automation was written for companies with a dedicated ops team, a software budget in the six figures, and someone on staff whose whole job is systems. That is not you. You have a dispatcher who also answers the phone, a lead tech who sometimes handles scheduling, and maybe an office manager who does everything else.

Generic automation advice tells you to "map your processes" and "identify bottlenecks." Useful, maybe. Practical, not really. What you need is someone to say: here is the exact tool, here is what it does, here is how long it takes to set up, and here is what breaks when you do it wrong.

That is what this post does.

## The Four Tools Worth Your Time

I am not going to list twenty options. Here are the four that actually get used by small trades and service operations, in order of how fast they pay off.

### 1\. Make.com

Make.com is the automation engine. Think of it like the wiring behind your panel box. It connects your other apps and tells them when to talk to each other. When a job closes in your field service software, Make can automatically send a review request by text, log the job in a Google Sheet, and notify your office manager in Slack, all without anyone touching a keyboard. Pricing starts at free and the paid tier that most small shops need runs about $9 a month.

Setup time for a basic job-close automation: two to three hours the first time. After that you copy the template.

### 2\. Claude

Not for writing blog posts. For the stuff your office manager spends 20 minutes on every day: turning a tech's voice notes into a readable job summary, drafting a follow-up email to a commercial prospect, writing a response to a bad Google review without sounding defensive. Give it a real prompt with real context and it saves real time. The $20-a-month Pro version is worth it.

### 3\. Otter.ai

Your lead tech does a site walkthrough, talks through what needs to happen, and drives away. Otter records and transcribes the whole thing from their phone. Back at the office, you paste that transcript into Claude and ask it to pull out a scope of work and a materials list. That is two manual steps replacing what used to be a 45-minute write-up. Otter runs about $17 a month for the business tier.

### 4\. Jobber or ServiceTitan

If you are already using one of these, you are probably not using half of what is already in there. Jobber has built-in automations for appointment reminders, follow-up requests, and quote expiration nudges. Most shops turn them on once and forget about them. That is fine. Set it and leave it. If you are not using either, Jobber is the right starting point for operations under 20 trucks. ServiceTitan makes more sense once you are bigger and need deeper reporting.

## A Real Automation Stack: What It Looks Like in Practice

Here is a working example from a roofing company with 11 employees. Before automation, their office manager was manually sending follow-up texts after every estimate, logging job statuses in a spreadsheet by hand, and copying customer info from their CRM into QuickBooks. It was taking her about 90 minutes a day.

The setup we built: Jobber triggers a follow-up text sequence automatically when an estimate goes out. Make.com watches for closed jobs and pushes the data to QuickBooks and a Google Sheet. Otter handles field notes on commercial inspections.

Total setup time was about six hours spread over two days. The office manager got back roughly an hour a day. She used it to actually call customers instead of doing data entry, and their close rate on estimates went up because follow-up got faster and more consistent.

No IT department. No consultant on retainer. One person who was willing to watch a few YouTube tutorials and spend a Saturday afternoon clicking through Make.com.

## What Actually Breaks (And How to Not Let It)

Automations fail when the trigger condition is wrong. In Make.com, if you tell it to fire when a job status changes to "complete" but your techs sometimes mark jobs "done" instead, the automation never runs. The fix is spending 30 minutes before you build anything making sure your team uses consistent status labels in your field software. That is the whole thing. Boring, but true.

The other common failure: nobody owns the automation. Build it, test it, then assign one person to check it weekly for the first month. After that it runs on its own. But that first month matters.
