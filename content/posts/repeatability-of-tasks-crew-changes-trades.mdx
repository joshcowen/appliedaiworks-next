The resources that actually ensure repeatability of tasks when your crew changes are: a short video library of your real processes (shot on a phone, not a studio), written checklists tied to each job phase, a simple knowledge base your dispatcher or foreman can search in 30 seconds, and one consistent offboarding habit that captures what a departing tech knew before they walk out the door. That is the whole system. Everything else is decoration.

Most trades businesses do not have a turnover problem. They have a documentation problem that turnover exposed. When your best installer leaves and suddenly nobody knows how you do the flatwork on a commercial pour, or which sequence you run the HVAC startup checks in, that knowledge did not disappear. It was just never written down in the first place.

According to [the Bureau of Labor Statistics Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/jolts.nr0.htm), construction and trade industries consistently run among the highest voluntary separation rates in the private sector. That is not changing. So the answer is not to hold onto people forever. The answer is to build systems that do not depend on any one person.

## What Actually Breaks When a Crew Member Leaves?

It is almost never the technical skill. You can hire for skill. What breaks is the tribal knowledge: the shortcuts that work, the customers who need extra patience, the sequence your team uses on a particular job type, the reason you switched suppliers two years ago. None of that is in any manual, because you never wrote a manual.

The goal is not to document everything. That will never happen, and it is not worth trying. The goal is to document the things that cause the most pain when they go missing.

## The Four Resources That Actually Hold Up

### 1\. Phone Videos, Not Training Manuals

A written manual takes forever to produce and nobody reads it. A two-minute phone video of your lead tech walking through a task gets watched. Record these on real job sites, not in a conference room. Narrate what you are doing and why. Name the specific tool. Show the mistake you are trying to prevent.

Store them in a shared Google Drive or a free Notion workspace, organized by task type. Label them clearly: "Condensate drain line flush, residential split system" beats "HVAC video 3." When the new hire asks how to do something, your foreman sends a link instead of stopping work to demonstrate.

This is not fancy. It works.

### 2\. Phase-Level Checklists, Not Step-Level Scripts

A checklist that covers every micro-step becomes a compliance exercise that people skip. A checklist that covers the five things that have to be true before you move to the next phase of a job actually gets used.

For an HVAC install, that might look like: pre-installation site verified, equipment matches work order, lineset routed and insulated, electrical rough-in confirmed, startup sheet completed and signed. That is it. Each person on the job knows what done looks like for their phase without a supervisor standing over them.

Build these in Google Docs first. If you want to get them into your field service software later, great, but do not wait for perfect software to start documenting.

### 3\. A Searchable Knowledge Base Your Team Will Actually Use

The bar here is low but specific: your dispatcher or a new tech should be able to find the answer to a common question in under a minute without calling anyone. That is the only test that matters.

Notion works well for this. So does a simple shared Google Doc with a table of contents. If you want something smarter, you can upload your process documents to [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com) and let it answer questions in plain English from your own materials. I have seen roofing companies do this with their install specs and shave a meaningful chunk of foreman interruptions per week.

The knowledge base does not have to be big to be useful. Twenty well-labeled documents covering your most common job types is worth more than two hundred poorly organized files nobody can find.

### 4\. An Offboarding Habit That Captures What Is Leaving

This one is the most overlooked and the highest value. When someone gives notice, you have a short window to pull out what they know. Do not waste it on paperwork and exit interviews about culture.

Sit them down with a recording tool like [Otter.ai](https://otter.ai) and ask them to talk through their most common tasks. What do they do that nobody else on the crew does? What would break in the first month after they leave? What do the new guys always get wrong? Record it, let Otter transcribe it, then edit that transcript into your knowledge base. An hour of their time before they go is worth weeks of figuring it out after.

## Where AI Fits Into This (and Where It Does Not)

AI does not solve the documentation problem on its own. You still have to do the thinking about what needs to be captured. But once you have raw material, AI speeds up the cleanup work considerably.

A transcript from Otter.ai can be dropped into Claude with a prompt like: "Turn this into a step-by-step checklist for a residential plumbing rough-in inspection. Plain language, no jargon." What would take a foreman two hours to write takes ten minutes. You still review it and fix what is wrong. But you are editing, not starting from nothing.

You can also use Make.com to automate reminders. A completed job form triggers a checklist reminder for the next phase. A new hire added to your system triggers a link to your onboarding video library. Simple automations that cost maybe an afternoon to set up and then run without anyone thinking about them.

## The Honest Truth About Why Most Shops Do Not Do This

It is not because they do not care. It is because the person who holds the most knowledge is also the busiest person on the team, and documentation always loses to the next job in the queue.

The way around this is to stop treating documentation as a project and start treating it as a habit attached to existing work. After every new job type, someone records a five-minute video. After every offboarding, someone runs the exit interview. After every callback that should not have happened, someone adds a line to the checklist.

Small additions, consistently, over a year. That is what a repeatable operation actually looks like.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the fastest way to document processes for a trades business?

Phone videos shot on the actual job site. A two-minute walkthrough of a real task, narrated by the person who does it best, is faster to produce than a written manual and more likely to get watched. Store them in a labeled Google Drive folder organized by task type. You can have a basic library running in a week without stopping regular work.

### How do you keep SOPs from going out of date in a trades business?

Attach updates to real events instead of scheduling a quarterly review nobody does. When a callback happens that should not have, add a line to the checklist. When a supplier changes a product, update the video note. When a tech finds a better sequence, record a two-minute addendum. Small updates tied to real work stay current. Standalone review cycles do not.

### Can AI help with onboarding new trades employees?

Yes, in specific ways. Tools like NotebookLM can answer a new hire's questions from your own uploaded documents instead of pulling from the internet. Claude can turn a recorded walkthrough transcript into a clean checklist in minutes. Make.com can trigger onboarding video links automatically when a new hire is added to your system. AI handles the formatting and delivery. You still supply the actual knowledge.

### What should you capture from an employee before they leave?

Ask them what they do that nobody else on the crew does, what would break in the first month after they leave, and what the new hires always get wrong. Record the conversation with Otter.ai, get the transcript, and edit it into your knowledge base. One hour of a departing employee's time, done right, can prevent weeks of figuring things out after they are gone.

### How many documents do you need in a knowledge base before it is useful?

According to Josh Cowen at Applied AI Works, twenty well-labeled documents covering your most common job types is worth more than two hundred poorly organized files nobody can find. The test is simple: can your dispatcher or a new tech find the answer to a common question in under a minute without calling anyone? If yes, the knowledge base is working. If not, the problem is usually organization, not volume.
