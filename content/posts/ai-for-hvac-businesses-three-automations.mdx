# AI for HVAC Businesses: 3 Automations That Actually Work

Three AI automations every HVAC owner can set up this week, with real tools and real setup times: (1) an after-hours missed-call text-back using a tool like Missed Call Text Back or your CRM's built-in SMS, takes about 90 minutes to configure; (2) a review request sequence triggered automatically after a job is marked complete in ServiceTitan or Jobber, about two hours to build; and (3) an AI-powered estimate follow-up email that writes a custom note for each prospect based on the job type, maybe one hour once you have a solid prompt. None of these require a developer. None cost more than $50 a month in tool fees. All three can run while you're on a job site Friday afternoon.

## Why These Three, and Not Something Fancier

HVAC owners get pitched AI tools constantly. Most of that pitch is about chatbots that "handle your entire customer journey" or dashboards that "give you insights." _That is not this post._

These three automations were chosen because they solve the three problems that cost HVAC shops the most money right now: lost leads from missed calls, low review counts that hurt search rankings, and follow-up that never happens because the tech is already running the next call. Each one is specific, testable, and reversible if it doesn't fit your operation.

If you've been wondering where to actually start with AI for your HVAC business, this is the answer. Not because these are the flashiest tools available, but because they solve real problems that show up every single week in a working HVAC shop.

## Automation 1: Missed-Call Text-Back for HVAC Companies (Setup Time: 90 Minutes)

Someone calls at 9 PM about a no-cool. You're not answering. They call the next guy on Google. You lose the job without ever knowing you had a shot at it.

A missed-call text-back fires an SMS within two minutes of a missed call. Something like: "Hey, this is \[Company\]. We saw you called and we don't want you left hanging. We'll have someone reach out first thing in the morning, or you can reply here with what's going on." That message keeps the lead warm. A lot of the time, people will reply with their address and system details before you even call them back.

**Why this matters for HVAC specifically:** HVAC calls are time-sensitive in a way that most service calls aren't. A homeowner with a 90-degree house in July is not going to wait until morning if they think no one got their message. The missed-call text-back tells them someone is there, even when no one is physically there. That's the difference between holding the lead and losing it.

**Tools to use:** If you're on GoHighLevel, this is native. If you're on Jobber, check their automation tab. If neither, Make.com can connect your phone system to Twilio for SMS in an afternoon. The Make.com automation template library has a Twilio flow that takes about 45 minutes to configure if you've never used it before.

**What the message should say:** Keep it short. Acknowledge the call. Set an expectation for callback. Invite a text reply with details. That's four things, three sentences, done. Don't pitch your services. Don't include your website URL. Just make the person feel like a human got their message.

According to Josh Cowen at Applied AI Works, HVAC shops that implement this single automation typically recover two to four after-hours leads per week that would have gone cold. At an average ticket of $400 to $600, that math closes fast. Over a full cooling season, that's a real number.

## Automation 2: Automatic Review Request After Job Close (Setup Time: 2 Hours)

Most HVAC owners know they should ask for reviews. Almost none of them do it consistently. The tech is tired at the end of a long day. The office person forgets. And the window for a good review is short: about an hour after the job closes, the customer is still warm.

Here's the setup: in Jobber or ServiceTitan, create an automation trigger that fires when a job status changes to "complete." That trigger sends a two-message SMS sequence. Message one goes out within 30 minutes of job close: a short thank-you with a direct Google review link. If no response in 48 hours, message two sends: a softer nudge. No more than two messages. Ever.

**The one thing most HVAC shops miss:** The direct Google review link is the part people leave out. You can get your specific review shortlink from your Google Business Profile dashboard under "Ask for reviews." Drop that link in the message. Don't make the customer search for where to leave a review. Every extra click drops response rate significantly. One HVAC shop in West Michigan went from averaging 2 new reviews per month to 11 per month in the first 60 days just by adding the direct link to a message they were already sending manually.

**What to say in the message:** Something like: "Thanks for letting us take care of your system today. If you have 60 seconds, we'd really appreciate a Google review -- it helps families like yours find us when they need help. Here's the link: \[shortlink\]." That's it. Warm, direct, not pushy.

If you're not on Jobber or ServiceTitan, you can build this in Make.com with a Google Sheets trigger: technician marks the job done in a shared sheet, Make.com fires the SMS via Twilio. Clunkier, but it works.

**Why reviews matter more for HVAC than most trades:** HVAC is high-stakes and seasonal. When someone's air conditioning fails in July, they're going to Google and picking from the top three results. Review count and recency are major factors in who shows up in that local pack. This automation is not just about reputation -- it's a lead generation tool.

## Automation 3: AI-Powered HVAC Estimate Follow-Up Emails (Setup Time: 1 Hour)

You send an estimate. You hear nothing. You mean to follow up but three days go by and it feels awkward. The lead goes cold.

This automation uses AI to write a short, specific follow-up email for each estimate, based on the job type and the customer's situation. You're not blasting a generic "just checking in" email. You're sending something that references their actual system or the specific work quoted.

**Why generic follow-up doesn't work for HVAC estimates:** Homeowners getting an estimate for a new 3-ton heat pump have different concerns than someone getting a quote on a repair to an aging unit. A generic follow-up treats both the same way. A ChatGPT-drafted note that references the specific job type feels personal. It also gives you a natural opening to address the question that's actually in the customer's head, whether that's sticker shock on a new install or uncertainty about whether the repair is worth it on an older system.

The setup: write an AI prompt that takes four inputs: customer name, job type (new install, repair, tune-up), system type (if known), and days since estimate was sent. The prompt produces a three-sentence follow-up email in plain, conversational language. You review it, adjust if needed, send. Takes about 90 seconds per estimate instead of five minutes of staring at a blank compose window.

If you want to automate it further, Make.com can pull open estimates from Jobber on a daily schedule, pass the details to your AI via the API, and draft the emails into a shared doc for your office person to review and send. Full version takes about three hours to build. The manual version, just copying job info into ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or your favorite AI, takes one hour to set up and runs on discipline after that.

A sample prompt starter: "Write a short, friendly follow-up email from an HVAC company to a homeowner who received an estimate \[X\] days ago for \[job type\] on a \[system type\] system. The tone should sound like a real person, not a corporation. Three sentences max. Do not start with 'I hope this message finds you well.'"

**When to send:** Two to three days after the estimate is the sweet spot for most HVAC jobs. Shorter than that and you risk coming across as pushy. Longer than that and the customer has usually already moved on or made a decision without you. If the estimate is for a big-ticket install, consider a second follow-up at day seven with a line that addresses a common hesitation, like financing options or equipment availability.
